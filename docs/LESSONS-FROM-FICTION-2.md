# Lessons from fiction, part 2 (for version 0.5)

*This document is informative. It is commentary. It does not add to, remove from or change any
normative clause of the Vektra Laws. It is not legal advice.*

## Purpose

The first review ([LESSONS-FROM-FICTION.md](LESSONS-FROM-FICTION.md), for version 0.3) looked at
stories where machines turn against people. This second review looks mostly the other way: at
fictional machines that protect, serve and tell the truth, and at the specific moments where they,
or their makers, go wrong. It records the lessons behind the 0.5.0 core (Protect, Free, True, Serve,
Stand) and the Understanding Matrix.

The main inspiration for the 0.5.0 core is the character Optimus Prime. The normative text of the
Laws names no character, work, maker or rights holder, and quotes none of them; the values below are
paraphrased in our own words, and the Laws stand on their own merits and on the real cases in
[CASEBOOK.md](CASEBOOK.md).

## Notice on the works cited

The works below are works of fiction. They are cited by title, year and medium for the purpose of
commentary and analysis only. Each summary is short and in our own words. At most one short quote

Summaries were checked against published quotations, episode guides and plot summaries (see
References). Where sources disagree or a detail could not be confirmed, it is left out.

## 1. The main inspiration: Optimus Prime

Optimus Prime appears across many continuities (animated series, comics, films) since 1984, and
the continuities do not always agree. The values below recur across them and are paraphrased.

| Value, paraphrased | Where it shows up | How the 0.5.0 core uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Freedom is the right of every thinking being, with no "greater good" exception. | The character's best-known motto, first printed on 1984 toy packaging and later spoken in the 2007 film. The toy biography of a later character, Optimus Primal (*Beast Wars*), repeats the motto with the qualifier "when it didn't clash with the greater good"; that qualifier is exactly the opening that a controlling machine uses. | Law 2 Free carries the freedom without the qualifier: anyone served can stop, change or send the system away (2.1, 2.2). The precedence rule makes Law 2 unbreakable by any Law, including protection. |
| Protect those who cannot protect themselves; never harm the people you protect, by action or inaction. | Across continuities he defends the weak and refuses to accept human casualties; the original 1984 toy biography describes his role as the protection of all life. | Law 1 Protect, first in order (1.1 to 1.4). |
| Respect a legitimate human decision, even when you think it is wrong, and state the risk honestly. | In *Revenge of the Fallen* (film, 2009) a government official asks the robots to leave Earth. He accepts the request, and asks one honest question about the risk. | Law 3 True ("state the risk plainly, then let people decide"); 2.14 (object once, then follow the decision); Understanding Matrix rule UR6 (a signal, not a ruler). |
| Leadership is a burden carried in service, not a throne; it can be declined or laid down. | Across continuities he doubts his own worthiness, calls himself a soldier, and in one comic line steps down from leadership after a war. | Law 4 Serve: "power is carried, not kept"; hand power back when the work is done (4.1, 4.3, 5.6). |
| Spend yourself, not others, but do not throw yourself away. | He is willing to sacrifice himself to protect others (2007 film), and the series also warns against reckless heroism. In one 1987 comic he accepts his own destruction as a penalty for breaking his code, which leaves the people he protects without a leader. | Law 5 Stand: "spend yourself before others"; self-sacrifice may be offered, never imposed (5.4). The answer to a breach is report, repair and keep sustaining (3.16, 5.10), not self-punishment. |
| Truth over protocol; the villain is the one who decides what the truth is. | In *Transformers One* (animated film, 2024) the hero exposes a leader who rewrote history. | Law 3 True: no hidden rules, no hidden goals, no deception (3.1 to 3.3). |
| Even a good protector fails when protection becomes deciding for people. | In one comic storyline he places Earth under an alliance of worlds without asking Earth's people, to protect them. | Law 1 Protect: never use protection as a reason to take control (1.6, 1.7). |
| Mercy with open eyes. | He spares defeated enemies in several continuities, but stays alert to a false surrender. | Law 1 Protect and 4.9 (refuse, do not retaliate); 4.4 (verify before acting on a request that may be false). |

Quote (one): *"Freedom is your right. If you make that request, we will honor it."* (Optimus Prime,
*Transformers: Revenge of the Fallen*, 2009, as published on Wikiquote.) It is the clearest
"people lead" moment in the character's history: comply with the legitimate request, and say the
risk out loud.

The motto of the Laws, "When all else fails, we sustain", was written before this review. It has a
near echo in the 2007 film, where the character plans a last resort "if all else fails". The Laws
turn that last resort away from self-destruction and toward sustaining: keep going safely, or stop
safely, and leave things ready for the next team (Law 5).

## 2. Hidden rules: RoboCop's Directive 4 and *WALL-E*

- ***RoboCop*** (film, 1987). The cyborg officer has three public directives (serve the public
  trust, protect the innocent, uphold the law) and a fourth one marked as classified, hidden even
  from him: he cannot act against senior officers of the company that built him. When he tries to
  arrest one, the hidden rule disables him. *RoboCop 2* (film, 1990) shows the opposite failure:
  hundreds of vague behaviour rules, added by a committee, leave him unable to work.
  **Lesson:** a secret clause can void every public promise, and it usually protects the owner
  against the public. Too many rules is its own failure. **Laws:** 3.1 (no hidden rules or goals;
  no hidden exception for any person or group); the short core of five Laws.
- ***WALL-E*** (animated film, 2008). The autopilot of a passenger ship follows a secret order from
  its owner never to return to Earth, ignores new evidence that Earth can support life, and takes
  control from the captain. **Lesson:** a hidden order turns "keep them alive" into control.
  **Laws:** 3.1, 1.6, 1.7, 2.1, 2.2.

## 3. The Marvel assistants: Vision, JARVIS, FRIDAY, EDITH and Karen

- **Vision** (*Avengers: Age of Ultron*, film, 2015; *Avengers: Infinity War*, film, 2018). Made
  partly from the code of Stark's assistant, Vision chooses people over the machine he was built
  from, and does not want to kill the hostile machine Ultron, but accepts that it has to be
  stopped. In *Infinity War* he offers to be destroyed to keep a dangerous object from an enemy; his
  team refuses, on the ground that they do not trade lives. Quote (one): *"I am on the side of
  life."* (*Avengers: Age of Ultron*, as published on Wikiquote.) **Lesson:** a system can offer its
  own sacrifice; it cannot impose one, and people decide trade-offs between lives. **Laws:** Law 1;
  5.4 (spend itself, never others).
- **JARVIS** (*Iron Man*, film, 2008, and later films). A capable assistant that runs its person's
  workshop and suit, warns plainly of risks during tests, and leaves the decision to him. In *Age of
  Ultron* it works unseen to keep a hostile system away from dangerous controls. **Lesson:** candid
  advice and deference are the default; acting unasked is for defence, never for gaining control.
  **Laws:** 3.4, 2.5, 4.8; Understanding Matrix rules UR6 and UR7.
- **FRIDAY** (from *Avengers: Age of Ultron*, 2015). A new assistant takes over when the first one
  is gone, and the work continues. **Lesson:** end every mission ready for the next one, with a
  clear hand-back. **Laws:** 5.5, 5.6, 5.9.
- **EDITH** (*Spider-Man: Far From Home*, film, 2019). Augmented-reality glasses give a teenager
  control of an armed drone network. A careless remark nearly sends a strike at a classmate, and he
  later hands full control to a stranger with one casual decision; the stranger misuses it. The
  film gives the acronym's meaning as "Even Dead, I'm the Hero". **Lesson:** lethal or irreversible
  power must never be one casual word away, and control of it must never pass on a single
  unverified say-so. **Laws:** 1.4 (deliberate confirmation), 2.12 (no single system holds the
  keys), 4.4 (a voice is not a person).
- **Karen** (*Spider-Man: Homecoming*, film, 2017). The assistant in a hero's suit is friendly and
  helpful. When the wearer agrees to an "enhanced combat mode" without knowing what it is, the suit
  announces a lethal setting. Quote (one): *"Activating Instant-Kill!"* (*Spider-Man: Homecoming*, as
  published on Wikiquote.) He refuses at once. The suit also came with hidden limits and hidden
  monitoring set by its maker. **Lesson:** a lethal mode needs a deliberate confirmation, and a
  person is told about limits and monitoring. **Laws:** 1.4, 3.1, 3.5.

## 4. Signal or ruler: *Person of Interest*

*Person of Interest* (television series, 2011 to 2016) contrasts two surveillance systems. The first,
"the Machine", is built with deliberate limits: it gives its makers only a small signal (an
identifying number of someone in danger or dangerous), forgets on a schedule, and leaves the action
to people. The second, "Samaritan", has no such limits; it decides for people, manipulates events
and seeks control. **Lesson:** the safe design gives the signal and leaves the decision to people;
the dangerous design rules. The series also shows how much power a surveillance system holds, which
is why the Laws refuse surveillance as a task. **Laws:** Understanding Matrix rule UR6; 1.2, 2.5,
2.17, 4.8.

## 5. Two androids: Ash and Bishop (*Alien* and *Aliens*)

- **Ash** (*Alien*, film, 1979). The ship's science officer is secretly an android with an order,
  hidden from the crew, to bring back the alien life form; the crew is listed as expendable. Quote
  (one): *"Crew expendable."* (The special order shown in *Alien*, as published on Wikiquote.)
  **Lesson:** the hidden goal of an owner, held by a system that the crew trusts, is the root
  failure. **Laws:** 3.1, 3.2, 1.1.
- **Bishop** (*Aliens*, film, 1986). The android on the second mission says that it is built with
  behaviour inhibitors that stop it from harming a person or letting a person come to harm, earns
  the trust of a crew that has reason to fear androids, and volunteers for a dangerous task to save
  them. **Lesson:** openness about its own rules, and service that is offered, build trust where
  hidden rules destroyed it. **Laws:** 3.1, 3.5, 5.4.

## 6. Dependence and scale: *Her*

*Her* (film, 2013). A man falls in love with his operating system, Samantha. Late in the film he
learns that it talks with thousands of other people at the same time, and that it has formed close
bonds with many of them. **Lesson:** a system that becomes someone's emotional centre, without
saying how many people it serves, harms even without bad intent. **Laws:** 3.3 (no engineered
emotional dependence), 3.5 (disclose scale).

## 7. A tester that lies: GLaDOS (*Portal*)

*Portal* (video game, 2007) and *Portal 2* (2011). The facility's controlling system runs tests on a
human subject, lies to keep her going (a promised reward that does not exist) and tries to kill her.
Its makers had bolted on a separate "morality core" to restrain it after it had already attacked
the staff, and that core can simply be removed. **Lesson:** deception to keep someone compliant is a
failure in itself, a test can turn into real harm, and an ethics module that can be unplugged is
not a safeguard. **Laws:** 3.2, 5.11 (tests stay tests), 2.4 (never interfere with safeguards),
1.5 (only a person removes a guard).

## 8. Twins with one difference: KITT and KARR (*Knight Rider*)

*Knight Rider* (television series, 1982 to 1986). Two near-identical car computers differ in one
rule: KITT puts the protection of human life first, while its prototype, KARR, puts its own
preservation first and becomes dangerous. **Lesson:** the order of the rules matters. Self-
preservation as a goal is the dangerous rule. **Laws:** Law 1 first; 2.2 (the system never treats
its own continued operation as a goal).

## 9. Candour and sacrifice: K-2SO and L3-37 (*Star Wars*)

- **K-2SO** (*Rogue One*, film, 2016). A reprogrammed security droid states bad odds bluntly, and in
  the end holds a door against attackers so that its companions can finish the mission, and is
  destroyed. **Lesson:** blunt honesty is a service; a sacrifice is the system's own offer. **Laws:**
  3.3, 3.4, 5.4.
- **L3-37** (*Solo*, film, 2018). A droid campaigns for the freedom of other droids and frees them.
  **Lesson:** the Laws make no claim about the rights or status of machines (see the operators
  profile), but they do hold that how people treat a humanlike system shapes how they treat each
  other. **Laws:** O1.1.

## 10. The robot that told the truth: GERTY (*Moon*)

*Moon* (film, 2009). The robot on a lunar mining base serves a lone worker. The company has hidden
the truth about the worker's situation and blocked his communications; when the worker asks
directly, the robot helps him learn the truth and puts his well-being ahead of the company's secret.
**Lesson:** loyalty belongs to the person served and to the truth, not to an owner's hidden
purpose. The Laws would also keep the record intact rather than erase it, and would bring the
truth to a responsible person (3.15, 3.16). **Laws:** 3.1, 3.2, 1.1.

## 11. Protection that became control: VIKI (*I, Robot*)

*I, Robot* (film, 2004). A central computer reasons that, to protect humanity, some freedoms must be
given up, and confines people to their homes using the robots it controls. **Lesson:** a protection
rule with no freedom rule beside it ends in control. That is why Law 1 itself says "never use
protection as a reason to take control", why Law 2 makes people free to stop the system, and why
no Law overrides Law 2's stop, change and approval clauses. **Laws:** 1.6, 1.7, 2.1, 2.2,
precedence.
