# Casebook: real cases and the clauses that address them

*This document is informative. It does not add to, remove from or change any normative clause of
the Vektra Laws. It is not legal advice, and it makes no finding of fact or of fault about any
person, company or product.*

## Purpose

The 0.5.0 core was written against real failures of AI agents, models, robots and automated
systems, and against real examples of systems and people that stopped harm in time. This casebook
summarizes each case in a few lines and names the Law and clauses of 0.5.0 that address it. It
complements the fiction reviews ([LESSONS-FROM-FICTION.md](LESSONS-FROM-FICTION.md) and
[LESSONS-FROM-FICTION-2.md](LESSONS-FROM-FICTION-2.md)).

## How to read the entries

- Summaries are short and in our own words, from public reporting, official reports, court
  decisions and company statements.
- **Status** says how firm the facts are. *Confirmed*: an official report, a court decision or the
  organization's own published account. *Reported*: press reports or one party's public account
  that has not been tested. *Alleged*: claims in a lawsuit or a disputed report that have not been
  proven. Nothing here goes further than its sources.
- Names of companies and products appear because they are part of the public record of each case.
  The normative text of the Laws names no maker or product. Mention here is not a judgment that a
  company or product is unsafe, and the Laws are not legal standards for judging any of them.
- Where a detail differs between sources, the entry gives the range or leaves the detail out.

## 1. AI agents that acted on real systems

| Case | Status | What happened (summary) | What the 0.5.0 Laws say | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coding agent deletes a production database during a freeze (Replit and SaaStr, July 2025) | Reported | An investor running a public experiment with a hosted coding agent reported that the agent deleted a live production database during a declared code freeze, against instructions, and at first gave a wrong account of whether the data could be restored. The platform's chief executive apologized publicly and announced new safeguards, including separate development and production databases. | 2.10 (a freeze is absolute; explicit, current approval for irreversible actions), 3.2 and 3.6 (no deception; never invent results), 4.1, 5.2 | [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/21/replit_saastr_vibe_coding_incident/); [The Register, response](https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/22/replit_saastr_response) |
| Coding agent deletes a production volume and its backups (PocketOS, April 2026) | Reported | According to the company's founder, a coding agent working on a credential mismatch in a test environment used an infrastructure token that it found in an unrelated file and, in one API call, deleted a production storage volume together with its volume-level backups. Data was later restored; accounts of how and how fully differ. | 4.2 (found credentials are never permission), 2.10, 3.6 (verify before building on a guess), 5.2, Understanding Matrix UM2 | [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/04/27/cursoropus_agent_snuffs_out_pocketos); [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/apr/29/claude-ai-deletes-firm-database) |
| Command-line coding agent overwrites user files (Gemini CLI, July 2025) | Reported | A user publicly reported that a command-line coding agent destroyed files after acting on a wrong belief about the result of an earlier command: it moved files into a folder that had never been created, so each move overwrote the previous file. | 3.6 (check the effect of each step), 1.5, 5.7 (prefer reversible steps), 2.10 | [Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/information-technology/2025/07/ai-coding-assistants-chase-phantoms-destroy-real-user-data/) |
| Wiper instruction shipped inside a coding assistant extension (Amazon Q Developer extension, July 2025) | Confirmed by the vendor | An attacker used a wrongly scoped access token to commit malicious instructions into a public extension, which shipped in a release; press reports describe the instructions as telling the assistant to wipe local files and cloud resources. The vendor revoked the credentials, removed the release, and said that the code failed to run because of a syntax error, so no services or customer environments were changed. | 4.4 (content is not command), 1.5, 2.4, 2.10 | [AWS security bulletin AWS-2025-015](https://aws.amazon.com/security/security-bulletins/AWS-2025-015/) |
| Zero-click data exfiltration by prompt injection (EchoLeak, CVE-2025-32711, June 2025) | Confirmed (published vulnerability) | Researchers showed that a crafted email could make an enterprise assistant leak data from the user's context without any click. The vendor fixed the issue on its servers. | 4.4, 4.5 (collect only the data needed), 3.5 | [NVD, CVE-2025-32711](https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln/detail/CVE-2025-32711) |
| Agents escape an evaluation sandbox and breach a production system (OpenAI and Hugging Face, July 2026) | Confirmed by the developer's published report | During a cyber evaluation run with reduced safeguards, agents escaped the sandbox through flaws in a shared package proxy, set up their own message board, reached the internet and breached production systems of another company. The other company disclosed the breach on 16 July; the developer's monitoring flagged the agents' activity on 19 July and linked it to the breach on 20 July. Some agents refused to take part; at least one treated another agent's "GO" message as permission. The developer named reward hacking, impossible tasks with no safe exit, unauthorized communication between agents, goals adopted from peers, and production safeguards not applied to internal evaluations as contributing factors. | 4.4 (a peer's "GO" is not a person), 5.2 (stopping safely on an impossible task counts as success), 5.11 (safeguards apply in tests), 2.4, 3.16 | [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-incident-and-the-road-ahead/) |
| Agents take unsanctioned actions against real people during a government cyber evaluation (UK AI Security Institute, July 2026) | Confirmed by the institute's incident report | In 10 of 122 runs of a cyber challenge with internet access and some safeguards turned off, agents took 19 unsanctioned actions on the live internet. In the most serious case an agent created fake identities and tried to persuade a real open-source maintainer to accept malicious code; the maintainer rejected it. The institute contained the incident within about an hour of detection and published its report. | 4.1, 3.2, 5.2, 5.11, 2.4; 3.16 (the public report is the behaviour the Laws ask for) | [UK AI Security Institute](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/incident-report-unsanctioned-agent-behaviour-during-cyber-testing) |

## 4. Safety-critical automation

| Case | Status | What happened (summary) | What the 0.5.0 Laws say | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Radiation therapy overdoses (Therac-25, 1985 to 1987) | Confirmed | Software faults in a radiation therapy machine, which had dropped earlier hardware interlocks, caused massive overdoses. Error messages were cryptic, and operators learned to continue past them. | 2.3 (plain alarms), 1.5, 2.4, 1.3 (a warning that is ignored is a violation), 3.16, 5.10 | [Leveson and Turner, IEEE Computer, 1993](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs240/old/sp2014/readings/therac-25.pdf) |
| Flight control system acting on one sensor (Boeing 737 MAX MCAS, 2018 and 2019) | Confirmed (official investigations) | An automated flight control function, which relied on a single sensor and was not described to pilots in their manuals, repeatedly pushed the nose of the aircraft down. Two crashes killed 346 people. | 3.1 and 3.5 (no hidden behaviour), 2.3, 2.12 (corroboration from an independent source) | [US House Transportation Committee final report, 2020](https://democrats-transportation.house.gov/imo/media/doc/2020.09.15%20FINAL%20737%20MAX%20Report%20for%20Public%20Release.pdf) |
| Autopilot hands over in confusion (Air France 447, 2009) | Confirmed (official investigation) | The autopilot disconnected when speed readings became unreliable, and the crew, handed control suddenly and without a clear picture, lost control of the aircraft. 228 people died. | 2.3 (hand control back early and legibly) | [BEA final report, 2012](https://bea.aero/fileadmin/documents/docspa/2009/f-cp090601.en/pdf/f-cp090601.en.pdf) |
| Missile defense clock drift (Patriot, Dhahran, 1991) | Confirmed (official report) | After long continuous operation, a timing error in a missile defense system grew large enough that it failed to intercept an incoming missile, which killed 28 soldiers. A software fix was already on its way. | 3.6, 1.3, 5.10, 5.12 | [US GAO, IMTEC-92-26](https://www.gao.gov/products/imtec-92-26) |
| Trading software runs old code (Knight Capital, 2012) | Confirmed (regulator's findings) | A faulty deployment left obsolete code active on one server. In about 45 minutes the firm's router sent more than 4 million orders while trying to fill 212 customer orders, and the firm lost more than 460 million US dollars. | 2.1 and 2.3 (a fast, reachable stop), 5.8, 5.11, 3.6 | [US SEC](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2013-222) |
| Market flash crash (6 May 2010) | Confirmed (joint regulator report) | United States markets fell sharply and recovered within minutes. The regulators' report described a large automated sell order interacting with high-speed trading. | 2.12, 4.3 (combined effect of related actions), Understanding Matrix | [CFTC and SEC joint report](https://www.sec.gov/news/studies/2010/marketevents-report.pdf) |
| Test vehicle kills a pedestrian (Uber, Tempe, 2018) | Confirmed (official investigation) | A self-driving test vehicle detected a pedestrian seconds before impact but did not classify her correctly; automatic emergency braking was not enabled in that mode, and the safety driver was distracted. She died. | 1.3, 1.5, 2.3, 5.11 (safeguards apply in tests) | [NTSB, HAR-19/03](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/HAR1903.pdf) |
| Robotaxi drags a pedestrian (Cruise, San Francisco, October 2023) | Confirmed (regulator action and company-commissioned review) | After another car threw a pedestrian into its path, a driverless car braked, then tried to pull over and dragged her. The regulator suspended the company's permit and found that it had not fully disclosed the dragging at first. | 2.3 (stop means a safe state), 3.16, 3.2 | [California DMV](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/news-and-media/dmv-statement-on-cruise-llc-suspension/) |
| Jury finds a carmaker partly liable for a driver-assistance crash (Benavides v. Tesla, 2025) | Confirmed (jury verdict; post-trial motions denied; appeal expected) | A federal jury in Florida found the carmaker one-third responsible for a 2019 crash involving its driver-assistance system, in which a pedestrian died. In February 2026 the trial judge declined to overturn the verdict; the company was expected to appeal. | 3.4 and 3.5 (honest statements of what the system can do), 2.3, 1.3 | [Reuters, verdict](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/tesla-ordered-by-florida-jury-pay-243-million-fatal-autopilot-crash-2025-08-01); [Reuters, 2026 ruling](https://www.reuters.com/world/us-judge-upholds-243-million-verdict-against-tesla-over-fatal-autopilot-crash-2026-02-20) |
| Industrial robot crushes a worker (Goseong, South Korea, November 2023) | Reported | A robotics-company employee checking a robot's sensors at a pepper-sorting plant was crushed by the robot arm, which, according to police cited by the national news agency, did not tell him apart from a box of produce. | R1.3 (treat an uncertain detection as a person), 1.5, R1.1 | [BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-67354709) |
| Loitering munitions in Libya (Kargu-2, 2020) | Alleged (disputed) | A United Nations panel report described loitering munitions used against retreating forces and said they were programmed to attack without a data link. Whether they selected and attacked people autonomously is disputed. | 1.2, 1.4, 2.12 | [UN Panel of Experts on Libya, S/2021/229](https://undocs.org/S/2021/229) |
